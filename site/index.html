Where this came from

The #1 AEM RFP requirement, and today it's the weakest surface

We reviewed 70+ AEM RFPs across 2026 Q1–Q4. In almost every one, translation / multi-site management / localization is a top requirement. In DA + Experience Workspace today that flow is poor. In a field working session we mapped nine concrete gaps against real customer requirements; Meridian is the prototype that answers them, built DA-native so it rides the edge platform rather than bolting a separate system beside it.

Honest read: enterprise TMS still wins on raw translation breadth (100+ languages, mature TM/glossary, project management) and scored highest in our teardown. Meridian isn't trying to out-translate them. It wins by being native to the edge platform, governing what's market-specific vs inherited, and surfacing exposure, so the content stays correct without a separate system.

The one idea everything rests on

One canonical page, typed market layers, variants computed fresh

Instead of copying a master and rolling out edits (and losing them on the next rollout), Meridian keeps one canonical content object per page and a stack of typed adaptation layers per market. The market page a visitor sees is a derived variant: computed from canonical + layers, never hand-maintained. Delete it, recompute, get byte-identical output.

Translate

Machine work, language layer only

Takes the English page's language content and machine-translates it. Clean results publish; low-confidence ones are held for a human. It scales: one action across many pages and markets.

Think: fast, automatic, reversible. The machine's job.

Localize

Translate + human-owned market content

Everything translate does, plus the segments a machine must never author on its own: price, offers, and legal / compliance. Those are withheld from the machine and authored or signed off by a person, per market.

Think: translation is a subset of localization. The human's job sits on top.

Why this split matters for the UX: it's the reason the surfaces diverge later. Translate bulks cleanly. The human parts of localize do not bulk the same way, and that's the crux of Jiawei's diagram below.

The part worth the most time

Market Rules: the typed layer stack

This is the real differentiator and the least self-evident surface, so it's worth a worked example. Each market is a set of typed layers applied over the canonical page in a fixed precedence. A block with no layer entry is inherited: it tracks the canonical and updates automatically. A block with an entry holds the market's own value. That's how "change one thing for Spain and keep everything else in sync" works without MSM's break-the-link problem.

Why it exists, from the RFP data

Market Rules isn't a model we liked on paper. Each layer answers a requirement that showed up repeatedly across the 70+ RFPs:

  • Regulated-market compliance. Banking, pharma, and insurance RFPs require locale-specific legal disclosures that legal owns and signs off, and that a machine must never author. This is the single most common hard requirement, and it forced the compliance layer plus the publish gate.
  • Price & offer localization. Commercial RFPs need per-market currency, offers, and regulated pricing, owned by the business and never auto-translated. That became the commercial layer.
  • Brand & terminology control. RFPs consistently ask for do-not-translate brand terms and approved glossaries per market, which is why the language layer is governed, not a raw MT dump.
  • Edit once, keep local edits. The recurring complaint about MSM: a rollout overwrites a market's customizations, or breaking inheritance to customize means it stops getting future updates. Inherit-by-default with typed overrides is the direct answer.
  • Governance & audit. Enterprise RFPs require who-changed-what and approval trails, which is why every entry carries a reason and provenance and writes an immutable version.

Worked example: a "Spring Refresh" offer page in Spanish (es-MX)

Canonical
The English source of truth: hero, offer price, legal block. Edited once. Every market inherits from here unless a layer overrides.
Language machine
Hero heading + CTA machine-translated to Spanish; high confidence, auto-applied. If confidence were low, it would route to Approvals instead of publishing.
Commercial human
The offer price is overridden for the Mexican market (currency, amount, terms). Withheld from the machine. A person sets it; the number is never machine-translated.
Compliance human · gated
The legal disclosure is the locally required Spanish version, owned by legal. Detected present or absent, never scored, never machine-authored. Absent = publish blocked.
Structural optional
A market may fork layout (add or drop a block) when it genuinely differs. An explicit escape hatch; a forked block stops tracking canonical by design.

Everything not named above stays inherited. Edit the canonical hero image next week and every market picks it up automatically, while Mexico keeps its own price and legal text. That inheritance model is what the Market Rules UI has to make legible: for any page and market, which blocks are inherited, which are held, and by whom (machine vs human vs legal).

What you do in this tab

The four interactions the prototype exposes, and what the design has to support:

Author a layer

Add, edit, or remove an override

Set a market's own value on any block, or clear it to fall back to inherited. Every entry records a reason and who made it (machine, author, or legal), so the stack stays auditable. Entries are immutable: a change writes a new version rather than overwriting.

Suggest layer

The assistant proposes, a human confirms

A classifier reads a block and proposes its layer type (language, commercial, compliance, structural). It only ever proposes. Nothing applies until a person accepts it, so the machine never silently reclassifies legal or price content.

Add a market

Describe it, review, activate

Describe the new market in plain language. Meridian proposes a starter policy (which layers it needs), you review and adjust the locale and rules, then Activate writes the policy plus an empty layer set ready to fill. No hand-editing of config files.

Compliance is locked

Human-only, non-publishable until signed off

Compliance entries carry a non-publishable banner and can only be authored by a person. The system can flag that a required compliance block is missing, but it will never write or suggest the content, and it blocks publish until the block is present.

The rule that constrains the design

A machine never authors commercial or compliance content, not even as a suggestion. Compliance is only ever detected present or absent. This isn't a nice-to-have: it's what makes Meridian safe for regulated industries (banking, pharma, insurance) that dominate the RFP set, and it's why "localize" always has a human in the loop.

The rest of the app, in brief

The supporting surfaces

These are largely self-explanatory in the prototype, so the short version:

Dashboard

Coverage at a glance

Every page × market in one matrix: stage (live staged none) and freshness (current / stale / missing).

RFP driver: "show me what's translated and what's out of date." Others: MSM has a rollout report but no freshness-vs-source at a glance.

Issues

Where you're exposed

A read-only radar of five finding types: stale, missing-required compliance, drift across markets, uncovered market, coverage gaps.

RFP driver: regulatory risk and "which markets are behind." Others: you normally find out when legal or a regional team complains.

Approvals

Human-in-the-loop gate

Clean translations auto-publish; low-confidence ones queue here; compliance-absent is a hard block. Approve, or reject with a reason.

RFP driver: governance and sign-off. Others: TMS review steps are heavyweight; MSM and DA have none.

Side-by-side preview

Compare source and localized, live

Click a page and its current state previews on the right straight away. During translate and localize, the English source and the localized version sit side by side, so review happens against the real rendered page, not an XLIFF file. The withheld price and legal segments show in context.

In-editor panel

Status where the author already is

Inside the DA editor, a reference panel shows where the current page lives in every market (live / staged / not localized) and deep-links into Meridian pre-scoped. The AEM "references" sidebar, reimagined for EDS.

SEO

Translated page slugs

Localized pages can publish at a translated URL (/es/financiacion, not /es/financing), with hreflang kept consistent. A concrete SEO win MSM doesn't give you, and a frequent RFP line item.

Answering your questions

Information architecture & the go-to use cases

Starting from Jiawei's model. It maps almost exactly onto how the prototype already splits, so it's a strong base. The one thing worth resolving is the right-hand circle.

Jiawei's model, redrawn. Three zones, one per use-case cluster.

The three zones, and what each is for

Overlap · in editor

Single page: both, together

The author is on the canvas. Translating and localizing one page is a single continuous act, so keep them together here. The in-editor panel and live side-by-side preview both live in this zone; the bulk zones work from status and coverage rather than per-page preview.

Left

Multi-page translate

The machine pass at scale: many pages × many markets in one action. Clean results publish, the rest go to Approvals. This zone bulks naturally.

Right · the contested one

Multi-page localize

Not bulk-authoring legal and price across 40 pages. It's the human-governed pass at scale: set a market's rules once, then work a review queue + coverage view.

Why "multi-page localize" isn't the opposite of translate

Silvia's instinct was right to question it. If "localize 40 pages" meant a machine writing 40 pages of legal and pricing, it would either break the no-machine-authored-compliance rule or leave 40 unfinished drafts. So that's not what it is. Multi-page localize is two real jobs:

1. Set the market's rules once: glossary, do-not-translate terms, required compliance blocks, price and date formats. These apply to every page translated into that market, so the human effort is defined once and inherited everywhere. 2. Work the queue + coverage: a portfolio view of every page × market with its status, where a reviewer opens an item, authors or approves its commercial / compliance, and promotes to live. The scale is in the governance and the queue, not in machine-authoring.

IA recommendation

  • Keep single-page translate + localize fused in the editor. One surface, in context, both actions.
  • Give multi-page translate its own bulk surface (the Pages view): select, translate, auto-publish clean, queue the rest.
  • Split multi-page localize into "market rules" + "queue/coverage," not a bulk-authoring button. This is the design decision that resolves the contested circle.
  • Dashboard, Issues, Approvals are cross-cutting read/act surfaces that serve all three zones; they don't need to live inside a zone.
